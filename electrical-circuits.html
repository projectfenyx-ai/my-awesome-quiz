Electrical Circuits

All electrical circuits can be divided into three basic types: series, parallel and combined.

Series circuits

The simplest of them is the series circuit. It is characterized by the fact that it has only one path for current flow.

First ruleThe current flow in the series circuit must be the same at any point in the circuit.
Second rule (a)The sum of the voltage drops around the circuit must be equal to the applied voltage.
Second rule (b)The total resistance is equal to the sum of the individual resistances.

Parallel circuits

Parallel circuits are characterized by the fact that they have more than one path for current flow.

There are three separate points from which current can flow: through resistor R1, resistor R2 and resistor R3. Since current can flow through each of these resistors,

the total current in a parallel circuit is the sum of the currents of the individual paths.
ITOTAL = I1 + I2 + I3

Notice that in the picture above, each of the resistors is connected directly across the incoming power line.

If a new component is added to a circuit, a new path of current flow is created, but total resistance is DECREASED.

Total resistance of a parallel circuit can be found by one of two formulas.

First formula

RT = (R1 × R2) / (R1 + R2)

Second formula

1/RT = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3

Combination circuits

A combination circuit contains both series and parallel connections within the same circuit.

Combination circuits are seldom, if ever, used, so only a brief description is given.

In the figure above, resistor R1 is connected in series to resistors R2 and R3, which, in turn, are connected in parallel to each other. It is obvious from the picture that the total current of the circuit will always flow through resistor R1 and, at the junction of resistors R2 and R3, will split into two separate paths. The amount of current that flows through each resistor is determined by its resistance.

A combination circuit must also be calculated by using Ohm’s Law formulas for series and parallel circuits.

1. Calculate the parallel parts of the circuit and transform them into one total resistor.
2. Then add to it all the series resistance of the circuit and, in this way, calculate the total circuit resistance.

Electrical quantities

Coulomb

A coulomb is a quantity measurement of electrons. One coulomb contains 6.25 × 1018 electrons. Since the coulomb is a quantity measurement, it is similar to a gallon or liter. It takes a certain amount of electrons to equal a coulomb.

Ampere

The ampere, or amp, is defined as one coulomb per second. Notice that the definition of an ampere involves a quantity measurement (the coulomb) combined with a time measurement (the second). One amp of current flows through a wire when one coulomb flows past a point in one second. The ampere is a measurement of the actual amount of electricity that is flowing through the circuit. In a water system, it would be comparable to gallons per minute or per second.

Volt

Voltage is actually defined as electromotive force, or EMF. It is a force that pushes the electrons through a wire and is often referred to as electrical pressure. But you should remember that voltage cannot flow. To say that voltage flows is like saying that pressure flows through the pipe. Pressure can push water through a pipe, and it is correct to say that water flows through a pipe, but it is not correct to say that pressure flows through a pipe. The same is true for voltage. The voltage pushes current through a wire, but voltage cannot flow through a wire.

Ohm

The ohm is a measure of the resistance to the flow of the current. The voltage of the circuit must overcome the resistance before it can cause electrons to flow through it. Without resistance every electrical circuit would be a short circuit. All electrical loads, such as lamps, motors, transformers, and so forth, are measured in ohms.

Watt

Wattage is a measure of the amount of power that is being used in a circuit. It is proportional to the amount of the voltage and the amount of current flow. To understand watts, return to the example of the water system. Assume that a water pump has a pressure of 120 psi (pounds per square inch) and causes a flow rate of one gallon per second. Now assume that this water is used to drive a waterwheel. Let’s say the waterwheel has a radius of 1 foot from the center shaft to the rim of the wheel. Since water weighs 8.34 pounds per gallon, and is being forced against the wheel at a pressure of 120 psi, the wheel could develop a torque of 1000.8 foot pounds:

120 × 8.34 × 1 = 1000.8

If the pressure is increased to 240 psi, but the water flow remains constant, the force against the wheel will double:

240 × 8.34 × 1 = 2001.6

Ohm’s Law

Ohm’s Law is named for the German scientist Georg Ohm. He discovered that all electrical quantities are proportional to each other and can therefore be expressed as mathematical formulas. In its simplest form, Ohm’s Law states that

IT TAKES 1 VOLT TO PUSH 1 AMP THROUGH 1 OHM

Assume that:

“E” stands for electromotive force and is used to represent voltage.
“I” stands for intensity of the current and is used to represent the amount of current flow, in amps.
“R” stands for resistance and is used to represent ohms.

Then the formula is:

E = I × R
I = E / R
R = E / I
VOLTAGE is equal to AMPS multiplied by OHMS
